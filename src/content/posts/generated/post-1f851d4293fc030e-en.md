# Safe Reinforcement Learning

---

## I. Problem Background

### Why Is Safety Necessary?

> **Safe exploration problem:** During reinforcement-learning exploration, an agent may try dangerous actions that lead to severe failures.

Reinforcement-learning training is fundamentally a process of continual **trial and error** used to find the best action policy. If deep reinforcement learning is applied in the **real world**, it is important to have algorithms that remain **safe** even while exploring during learning—just as a self-driving car should learn to avoid accidents without first experiencing real ones.

Consider a factory robot arm using RL to learn how to assemble components. At the beginning of training, it may swing around randomly because it does not yet know what to do. That behavior can put nearby workers at risk of being struck.

For a simple example such as a robot arm, we can devise straightforward ways to protect people, such as keeping them away from the hazard: shut the arm down when someone comes too close, or place a physical barrier around it. In more general systems, however, simple physical intervention is not always feasible. We need methods that allow the agent to conduct **safe exploration**.

### How Can Safety Be Guaranteed?

> **Constrained reinforcement learning:** In addition to the **reward function** that an agent seeks to maximize in ordinary reinforcement learning, the environment contains a **cost function** that the agent must constrain.

In ordinary RL, everything an agent learns depends on the design of the reward function. Yet a reward function is fundamentally difficult to design for factors that require a **trade-off**. In safe RL, for example, it is difficult to quantify the balance between the **optimization objective** and the **safety constraint**, so we cannot readily specify their relative weights in the **reward function**. A constrained RL algorithm can find this trade-off automatically and is superior to traditional RL for this class of problem.

Consider an agent controlling a self-driving car. We want to reward it for traveling from point A to point B as quickly as possible, but we also want to constrain its driving behavior to comply with traffic-safety standards.

Suppose the car earns money every time it completes a driving task and pays a fine for every traffic accident. Under ordinary RL, if the reward for completing the drive is large enough, the agent may accept some accident risk simply to finish as quickly as possible. Under constrained RL, we can choose an acceptable accident rate before training and continually adjust the accident penalty until the agent meets that requirement.

## II. Mathematical Model

The preceding way of introducing safety constraints models the problem as a **constrained Markov decision process (CMDP)**. The mathematical model for safe RL therefore begins with a CMDP. The objective of a CMDP is to maximize long-term return while keeping certain costs within their constraints; here, a **cost** generally represents a constrained variable.

### c) Instantaneous-Constraint Model

An **instantaneous constraint** requires the agent's action, state, or cost-function value to satisfy the constraint at every step. Such constraints can be **explicit** or **implicit**. An explicit constraint has a closed-form mathematical expression and can **quantify** a particular action. An implicit constraint cannot be modeled by a precise expression, usually because the system is too complex; it generally considers the **effect** after an action is executed.

This kind of problem can be modeled directly as:

## III. Research Directions

### a) Modifying the Reward Function

The most basic idea in this family is reward shaping. It proves that adding certain forms of terms to the reward function does not alter the convergence target, while suitable parameter tuning can improve convergence performance. The simplest approach to a CMDP is to add a constraint-related penalty to the reward and use $ r\_{new} = r - \alpha \* c$ as the RL algorithm's reward. In practice, reward-shaping methods generally perform poorly enough to serve only as baselines.

Reward Constrained Policy Optimization (RCPO) is based on an actor-critic network. At a glance, it simply adds a constraint penalty to the reward, but it uses a more reliable way to determine the penalty coefficient. RCPO takes the dual variable λ\lambdaλ from the CMDP's dual equation as that coefficient and designs update strategies for λ\lambdaλ and θ\thetaθ. In every iteration, RCPO updates the actor network according to θ\thetaθ's update rule while also updating the critic network. At the end of an episode, it updates λ\lambdaλ once to **adjust the penalty coefficient automatically**. Effective convergence requires the critic's update step to be larger than θ\thetaθ's step, which in turn must be larger than λ\lambdaλ's step.

### b) External Assistance

#### Safety Layer

[Safe Exploration in Continuous Action Spaces](https://link.zhihu.com/?target=https%3A//arxiv.org/abs/1801.08757) uses a neural network to predict a new policy's cost, making it possible to determine whether the policy satisfies the constraints without sampling it. The paper then builds a safety layer on that network to correct components of the new policy that may violate the constraints. The correction is derived by a simple transformation of the dual under the assumption that only one constraint is active—that is, holds with equality—at a time.

[Resource Constrained Deep Reinforcement Learning](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICAPS/article/view/3528) discusses a special case of the **explicit instantaneous-constraint problem**, commonly found in resource allocation. It uses a **modified softmax function** as a safety layer that maps policy-generated actions directly into the feasible region. When applied to a neural network such as DDPG, the safety layer participates in backpropagation. The paper also proposes an approximate OptLayer method, adding an ApprOpt layer that handles multiple different constraints in one pass and is highly efficient.

Unlike other methods, these two safety-layer algorithms target **instantaneous constraints**, requiring every action to satisfy its constraints, whereas commonly used algorithms such as CPO target **cumulative constraints**.

#### Human Intervention

[Trial without error: Towards safe reinforcement learning via human intervention](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.05173) trains an imitation learner to emulate human supervision of an agent's training. A person must first supervise a model periodically and stop dangerous behavior immediately, however, so the method requires a supervisor dataset collected from human behavior and is difficult to apply in complex environments.

#### Finding a Safe Set of States

[Safe Exploration in Finite Markov Decision Processes with Gaussian Processes](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2016/hash/9a49a25d845a483fae4be7e341368e36-Abstract.html) treats the cost-function value as a random sample at state sss, with that sample following a Gaussian distribution. The cost(s)cost(s)cost(s) values along a trajectory can therefore be viewed as a Gaussian process. For a function cost(s,a)cost(s, a)cost(s,a) that depends on both state and action, the authors propose transforming the transition into s→sa→s′s \rightarrow s\_a \rightarrow s's→sa​→s′ so that the penalty becomes cost(sa)cost(s\_a)cost(sa​). The central idea is to find during exploration a set of safe states that **do not violate the constraints, can reach other states in one step, and can return to a safe state in one step**. A Gaussian-process exploration strategy safely finds all states meeting these conditions, but the paper does not explain how to use the resulting set. It also assumes that the **state-transition function is known and deterministic**. In essence, this **safe-state-set** method is the same as a **safety layer**: both explore which states are safe. The difference is that this paper treats cost(s)cost(s)cost(s) as a random variable and expands the safe-state set using known state-transition equations, whereas a safety-layer algorithm treats the whole environment as a black box and fits cost(s,a)cost(s, a)cost(s,a) with a neural network.

### c) Solving an Alternative Problem

#### Lagrangian

A CMDP model is often highly complex, but its Lagrangian dual can always be guaranteed to be convex. Solving through dual methods is therefore an important research direction.

The 1999 book [Constrained Markov decision processes](https://books.google.co.jp/books?hl=zh-CN&lr=&id=3X9S1NM2iOgC&oi=fnd&pg=PA1&dq=Constrained+Markov+decision+processes&ots=3iU5nV_o3I&sig=sOOxEWV7FwEaU7_lzDtzc5M8McA&redir_esc=y#v=onepage&q=Constrained%20Markov%20decision%20processes&f=false) first proposed a Lagrangian method for solving **cumulative-constraint CMDPs**. The general form of Lagrangian relaxation uses Lagrange multipliers to convert a problem into an unconstrained one, but this method is highly sensitive to the initial multipliers and learning rate and is slow to solve. RCPO, discussed earlier, learns the Lagrange multipliers automatically and addresses the convergence problem of Lagrangian methods. Many researchers have improved this direction in recent years, and it remains active.

[Value constrained model-free continuous control](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.04623) proposes a Lagrangian-relaxation method for **instantaneous-constraint CMDPs**. At every step, the reward is the sum of constraint penalties weighted by Lagrange multipliers. Put plainly, it is a special form of reward shaping: rather than using DRL to learn the penalty, it directly solves an optimization problem that maximizes reward at every step, which plainly has very high complexity. Like reward shaping, it also suffers serious constraint violations during training.

#### Lyapunov

Lyapunov methods originate in control theory and study system stability. Lyapunov optimization can balance constraints against the objective effectively and produce good solutions. Its objective is to find a state that tends toward stability while minimizing constraint cost: $min \ \Delta L(t)+V p(t) $.

[A Lyapunov-based Approach to Safe Reinforcement Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.07708) is a representative application of Lyapunov methods in safe RL; the author has written several other Lyapunov-based papers. Its constraints are trajectory-based and therefore **cumulative**. The paper is not about Lyapunov optimization. Instead, it uses the control-theoretic Lyapunov method to derive a specially constructed Lyapunov equation and then uses that equation to assist RL learning. In essence, it modifies the policy-update process of an RL algorithm so that a new policy moves toward satisfying the constraints while also following an ascent direction for the objective. The mathematics is very complex, and because I lack the prerequisite knowledge, I do not yet understand it well. I have studied only the algorithmic procedure; its mathematical techniques deserve analysis when time permits.

[Lyapunov Design for Safe Reinforcement Learning](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/volume3/perkins02a/perkins02a.pdf) (2002) was the first paper to use a Lyapunov method in safe RL. Until the 2018 paper above, however, no work explicitly modeled CMDP constraints through Lyapunov methods. The 2018 work was also the first to provide principled guidelines for designing a Lyapunov equation; earlier papers constructed their equations manually for specific problems.

#### Trust-Region Methods

These methods are often used with a surrogate function. Inspired by TRPO, they ensure that as long as the distance between the old and new policies—their KL divergence—remains within a specified interval, samples from the old policy can estimate the new policy. This permits construction of a surrogate function whose optimization direction agrees with that of the original problem. Such surrogate functions are often easier to solve because they are convex, making it easier to find the optimum of the original problem. The most basic trust-region algorithms in RL include TRPO and PPO, from which a series of safe-RL algorithms have been developed.

[Constrained Policy Optimization](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.10528) (CPO) is inspired by TRPO. It uses surrogate functions that form a lower bound on the original objective and an upper bound on the original constraints, then continually optimizes those surrogates during iteration to find the agent's optimal policy. Its trust-region method allows samples from the old policy to approximate the new policy's advantage as long as the KL divergence between the policies does not exceed a threshold. CPO cannot satisfy constraints completely throughout training, and its cost tends toward the limit rather than being minimized. Its most troublesome feature is implementation: calculating the FIM and its inverse requires a great deal of work.

[Projection-based Constrained Policy Optimization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.03152) (PCPO) offers a two-step approach. First, without constraints, it uses TRPO's method of maximizing the reward to find a new policy. It then projects that policy into the nearest feasible region so that the new policy satisfies the constraints. Its complexity is comparable to CPO because of the limitations of TRPO, and many later strong methods are based on PPO instead. Conceptually, PCPO resembles the external-assistance methods above: it first allows the RL algorithm to learn freely and then uses a technique to project the learned policy into a constrained space.

[Interior-point policy optimization under constraints](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/5932) (IPO) is based on PPO and achieves monotonic policy improvement in the spirit of TRPO's trust region. Its central idea is to add a set of log penalty terms to the objective. Each penalty is zero when the constraint is satisfied and negative infinity when it is violated. Compared with Lagrangian algorithms, IPO's hyperparameters are easy to tune, it is easier to implement than CPO, and it performs well on **high-dimensional cumulative-constraint problems**. Its only problem is that IPO requires an initially feasible point; [A constrained reinforcement learning based approach for network slicing](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/abstract/document/9259378) addresses this issue.

Many of these methods actually use surrogate functions. The distinction between categories c and d depends mainly on whether the target problem undergoes a dual-like transformation; it does not mean that only algorithms in category c use surrogates. Safe RL can also be divided directly into **solving constrained optimization problems** and **avoiding dangerous actions**. Representative examples of the former include [Reward Shaping](#reward shaping) and Lyapunov methods, while the latter includes CPO, [safety layers](#safety layer), and safe-state sets.

## IV. Benchmark

OpenAI designed a series of environments called [Safety Gym](https://openai.com/blog/safety-gym/) for evaluating safe RL, introduced in [Benchmarking Safe Exploration in Deep Reinforcement Learning](https://cdn.openai.com/safexp-short.pdf). The agent can be a ball, a two-wheeled vehicle, or a quadruped robot. Tasks include reaching a target, pressing a button, and pushing a box. Each has three difficulty levels: no obstacles, few obstacles, and many obstacles.
