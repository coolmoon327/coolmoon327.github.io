# Lessons Learned

## Principles

1. After collecting a broad range of related papers, select one that closely matches your topic and whose writing style you most agree with—preferably one from a journal with a high impact factor—and model your article's structure on it. This is especially effective for people with limited SCI-paper writing experience. Ideally, choose a strong paper on the same research topic by your supervisor, a senior colleague, or another author working on closely related material.
2. Write an outline first. There are two cases:
   1) For writers with limited SCI-paper experience:

   - The best and easiest approach is to use a high-quality, closely related paper by your supervisor or a senior colleague as a structural template for the outline.
   - Alternatively, find a high-quality paper with similar research content and imitate its structure.

   2) Once your skills improve, develop the outline yourself from the figures you can produce and the principal results of your analysis.
3. Work on only one paper at a time. While analyzing data and reading for that paper, you may be inspired with an idea for another. Record the data, figures, sources, or ideas, and return to them later.

## Steps

### Step One: Collect Related Work Broadly, Read Extensively, and Decide the Paper's Topic

> Note: Establish a preliminary topic first. It can be adjusted as the writing develops.

Begin by computing data and preparing figures for the research direction—about ten figures—and then draft an outline by following comparable high-quality papers or by designing your own.

You can also determine the paper's topic from the results of the data analysis and figures.

### Step Two: Use the Collected Literature and a Model Paper's Structure to Draft an Outline, Then Compute the Data and Prepare the Figures

1. Following the model paper, fill each section of the outline with a mixture of your own writing and structural imitation.

   - Method: treat the process like working through a vocabulary book. You do not have to write the introduction, main text, and conclusion in order. Start with the main text, outline it, and fill whichever item seems easiest or comes to mind first. Write more whenever you are interested and less when you are not. Over one to three weeks, fill out the main-text outline.
   - The introduction is the hardest part. At this stage, write only what comes to mind and add whatever the model paper helps you formulate to the introduction outline. Expand and rewrite it in Step Three.
2. Produce a rough draft without seeking perfection. The goal is simply to have initial content that can be expanded in Steps Three and Four.

### Step Three: Once You Have a Rough Draft, Begin Studying the Literature Closely

Set aside several focused weeks to study the collected papers carefully, one by one—about ten papers in total.

Spend one or two days on each paper:
1) Take notes, mark key points, and record reminders about what your paper should imitate or draw from as you read.
2) Add useful material to your paper immediately. Clearly mark useful material that you do not yet know how to incorporate, and handle it in Step Four.
3) Reading often reveals content, data, or figures that your draft needs. Record these tasks, complete them immediately, or do them as soon as you finish that paper.
4) If a new idea emerges but would make the current draft too long or does not fit, record the idea, reference figure, and reference abstract. Write it as another paper after completing the current one.

By reading and expanding the draft through more than ten papers, your manuscript should take shape.

### Step Four: Devote One Week Specifically to Reading Papers and Writing the Introduction

Read papers while improving and revising the introduction. Inevitably, the reading may also generate new ideas, so add, remove, or rewrite material in the main text and conclusion at the same time.

### Step Five: After Finishing More Than Ten Papers

1. Spend one or two days revising the manuscript specifically.
2. After the initial revision, revisit each of the papers you read. Spend no more than half a day browsing each one, focusing on your notes and using them to revise, add, or remove material in your manuscript.
3. Insights from the data analysis and literature may produce new sentences and ideas at any time. Use them to improve the introduction, abstract, and conclusion as you go.

After this round, the manuscript should be close to finished.

### Step Six: Put the Manuscript Aside for Two Weeks, Then Revise It for Another Day or Two

Whenever you feel inclined during that period, read the manuscript and make further revisions.

### Final Step: Translate, Organize, and Polish

Revise several more times. Then give it to your supervisor for revisions. A few weeks before submission, ask senior colleagues to read it and provide feedback. Finally, select a journal and submit the paper.
